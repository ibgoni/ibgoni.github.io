Wired for Growth? Broadband Internet and Local Development in Africa

Draft available

Draft (PDF)
Abstract

Does connecting a region to the global internet raise local economic activity, and where do the gains land? I study the 2009–2012 arrival of submarine fibre-optic cables across seventeen Sub-Saharan economies, comparing grid cells within ten kilometres of the pre-existing terrestrial backbone with cells of the same country ten to twenty kilometres away, before and after each country’s first submarine cable, with a heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimator. The average effect is modest, 0.7 percent on local GDP and 7.3 percent on night lights, but it is concentrated along the inland chain that carries the bandwidth to users: it is about five times larger in cells within two kilometres of a 3G or 4G tower that delivers the backbone’s bandwidth over the air, and close to zero in cells far from a road. The result holds under a future-placement design that uses later-connected cells as controls, a recentering on predicted backbone exposure, exclusion of major-urban cells, and propensity-score matching. At the household level, working-age adults move out of subsistence farming into non-farm work, with no rise in overall employment and suggestive gains in consumption and assets. Digital infrastructure pays off as a system, not as a single asset.

Collusion in Willingness to Pay Elicitation Mechanisms

with Sherry Banyout (ENS de Lyon), Pierre Biscaye (Université Clermont Auvergne), Jules Gazeaud (Université Clermont Auvergne), and Nada Rostom (University of Antwerp)

Data analysis in progress

Abstract

The Becker–DeGroot–Marschak (BDM) mechanism is widely used in experimental and field settings to elicit individuals’ willingness to pay (WTP) for goods and services. Under standard assumptions, truthful reporting is the dominant strategy, making the mechanism attractive for measuring demand. The BDM mechanism is also increasingly used in selective trials to identify the causal impact of goods or services, where actual purchase is instrumented by the randomly assigned price (Chassang et al., 2012). In the course of implementing a BDM mechanism to allocate job-matching services in a field experiment in Egypt on young farmers’ adaptation to climate change, we identified a potential concern that has received little attention in the literature: collusion between enumerators and participants. Such collusion could arise for several reasons. For instance, participants who do not win the auction in the BDM may regret bidding too low and request that enumerators increase their reported willingness to pay in order to buy the service. Conversely, participants who are unable to pay for the service after winning the auction may ask enumerators to adjust their reported willingness to pay in order not to buy the service. Enumerators may have incentives to accommodate such requests, for example, to reduce the likelihood of reporting payment failures or disputes to supervisors, or simply to ease interactions with participants. Such collusion can bias estimated willingness to pay and compromise the identification strategy in selective trials. This project investigates the extent and implications of this issue. First, we conduct a systematic review of the studies using a BDM mechanism to document whether and how the possibility of collusion is discussed. Second, we propose a statistical test to detect collusion. Third, we implement the test across all studies using BDM with publicly available data. Fourth, we introduce a BDM protocol that is robust to collusion between enumerators and participants, and implement it in our field experiment in Egypt. Finally, we show how in our field experiment estimates of the impacts of the job-matching services would have differed had we not implemented this robust protocol.

When the Internet’s Backbone Breaks: Submarine Cable Disruptions and Worker Access to Global Online Labor Markets

with Joël Cariolle (FERDI)

Data analysis in progress

Abstract

Digital trade is often framed as weightless, yet worker-side access to global online labor markets may still depend on fragile cross-border internet infrastructure. I study that dependence by constructing a new audited global database of submarine cable disruptions and merging it to daily worker-country observations from the Online Labour Index. The worker panel covers 214 countries from June 2017 to September 2024 and overlaps with 103 verified or partially verified submarine disruptions in 63 countries. In country-by-day Poisson models with country and day fixed effects, observed worker-side platform participation falls by about 7 percent within 14 days of a disruption and by about 11 percent over the reported outage or repair window. Effects are much larger for multi-cable events and are concentrated in software, creative, and sales occupations, while buyer-country activity does not decline. I interpret these estimates as reduced-form evidence that the reliability of international connectivity is a binding short-run input into worker access to global digital labor markets.
